Q1. In Wisconsin, the move-over law requires you to change lanes, or slow down, when passing:

Move to a lane that is not next to the stopped vehicle when you safely can. When you cannot, slow down. People working at the roadside are struck every year by drivers who did neither.

Q2. After passing another vehicle, you should return to your lane when:

Seeing both headlights of the vehicle you passed in your mirror means you have enough room to move back over without cutting the driver off.

Q3. A conviction for driving under the influence usually results in:

Besides the suspension, fines and possible jail, a DUI stays on your driving record for years, and insurers usually raise premiums sharply.

Q4. Excessive speeding, well above the limit, is treated more seriously because:

Kinetic energy rises with the square of speed, so a small increase in speed is a large increase in damage and stopping distance.

Q5. In Wisconsin, you become unable to drive if you accumulate how many demerit points within any 12-month period?

Wisconsin suspends or revokes your driving privilege once you reach 12 or more demerit points within any 12-month period.

Q6. In Wisconsin, during the restricted period, a young driver may carry:

Crash risk for a teen driver climbs sharply with each additional teen passenger, which is what this rule is built around.

Q7. A space cushion around your vehicle is useful because:

Space is time. The more of it you keep on all four sides, the more options you have when something goes wrong.

Q8. Driving into low sun glare, the best response is to:

Glare hides brake lights and pedestrians. Slow down, clean the inside of your windshield, and leave more room.

Q9. In Wisconsin, using a hand-held cell phone while driving is:

There is no statewide hands-free requirement on ordinary roads, but hand-held use in a marked work zone is prohibited and texting is banned for all drivers.

Q10. You are in the left lane of a highway and a line of traffic builds up behind you. You should:

The left lane is for passing. Once traffic stacks up behind you, moving right is both safer and, in many states, required.
