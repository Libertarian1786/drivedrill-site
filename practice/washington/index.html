Q1. At night, pedestrians are hardest to see:

Most pedestrian deaths happen after dark. Lower your speed so your stopping distance fits inside your headlight range.

Q2. Blood alcohol concentration depends on:

Two people drinking the same amount can end up at very different levels, so nobody can judge their own BAC reliably.

Q3. Two lanes merge into one because of road work. The most efficient approach is to:

The zipper merge keeps queues shorter and speeds more even. Take turns at the merge point rather than fighting over it.

Q4. In Washington, if an adult in the front seat is not wearing a seat belt:

This is a primary enforcement state: no other violation is needed. The belt is also the single most effective thing in the car for surviving a crash.

Q5. In Washington, once you are in a shared center left-turn lane, you should not travel farther than:

Washington's guide says drivers shouldn't travel farther than 300 feet in a shared center turn lane, since the lane is only for entering or leaving traffic, not for passing.

Q6. Your right wheels drift onto a soft shoulder. You should:

Yanking the wheel at speed flips the car back across the road. Slow first, then ease back on with a gentle steering input.

Q7. On a multi-lane highway, slower traffic should use:

Keep right except to pass. Blocking the left lane forces other drivers to pass on the right, which is where crashes happen.

Q8. In Washington, leaving a child under age 12 unattended in a parked vehicle is a crime:

Washington makes it a crime to leave a child under 12 unattended in a parked vehicle at all, and separately a crime to leave a child under 16 unattended with the motor running.

Q9. In Washington, during the restricted period, a young driver may carry:

Crash risk for a teen driver climbs sharply with each additional teen passenger, which is what this rule is built around.

Q10. A motorcycle is entitled to:

Never share a lane with a motorcycle. Riders move within the lane to avoid road hazards and to stay visible.
